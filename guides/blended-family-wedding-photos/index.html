Blended families and step-parents in wedding photos

A blended family needs two decisions before the day: which parents stand together, and where each step-parent goes. Here are three ways to set it up, with the photo count each one gives.

The family we planned

To make this concrete, we used one blended family for every number on this page. Emma's mom has remarried, to Mark. Her dad has remarried, to Lisa. Emma has a sister, Kate, and a half-brother, Sam, who is 12. Her grandmother is coming. On James's side, his parents Diane and Paul are married, and he has a brother, Chris. Grandma and Sam are marked "needs to go first".

That's ten relatives. How many photos they need depends on one question: who is comfortable standing next to whom.

One family

If the four parents are on good terms, this is the simplest and the shortest: every parent photo has all four of them, and Emma's side needs only five photos plus the one with both sets of parents. Many blended families are exactly this. Don't add rules you don't need just because the family is blended.

Two households

Each parent appears with their own partner and never with the other household. Every group with parents in it is taken twice: once with Mom and Mark, once with Dad and Lisa. Kate, Sam and Grandma are in both versions. Where it saves time, the planner takes the two versions back to back (photos 4 and 5, and 8 and 9 here), so the swap is two people out and two in while everyone else holds still.

Exes only: the trap

This is the setup people choose by accident. You tell the planner that Mom and Dad never stand together, and stop there. Mark and Lisa have no rules, so they're welcome in every photo. The result: "Emma with parents, with Dad" comes out as Mark, Lisa, Dad and Emma. Mom's new husband is standing next to her ex-husband in a photo Mom isn't in.

Same count as two households, completely different photos. If you're keeping exes apart, decide where each new partner goes, too.

Why it takes four rules, not three

The obvious fix is three rules: Mom and Dad apart, Mom and Lisa apart, Dad and Mark apart. That looks complete, but it leaves Mark and Lisa free to stand together, and the planner (which never leaves anyone out of a group they belong to) gives you a third version of each parent group: Mark and Lisa, with neither Mom nor Dad. With three rules this family came out at 19 photos and 38 minutes, five of them with the two step-parents together and no parent in sight.

Add the fourth rule, Mark and Lisa apart, and it drops back to 14 photos, all of them the ones you meant. If the odd photos do turn up, you can also just untick them.

Step-siblings, half-siblings and your own kids

  • Half-siblings are brothers and sisters. Enter them that way and they're in every sibling and immediate-family photo, in both households' versions.
  • Step-siblings you grew up with are brothers and sisters too. Step-siblings you met at the engagement party fit better as "Other": they'll be in the big group with everyone on that side, not in the parents-and-siblings photos.
  • A step-parent who raised you is a parent in every way the photo list cares about. Enter them as "Step-parent / parent's partner" and they're in the parent photos.
  • Your own children, from this relationship or an earlier one: add them, tick "needs to go first", and add a photo of your own, "Couple with our kids". Kids are the people who most need to be done early.

Where everyone stands

A layout most photographers will recognise: the couple in the middle, each parent next to their own child, and a step-parent on the outside of the parent they came with. In two-household photos, keep the same layout for both versions, so the two frames look like a matching pair rather than a before and after. Siblings fill in around the parents; half-siblings stand with the household they live in, if that's easier for them.

Ask before the day, one parent at a time. The planner can split any group, but it can't tell you whether your dad minds standing near Mark. A two-minute private question to each parent settles which of the three setups you're in, and it's much better asked months ahead than at the altar.

Questions

How do you handle step-parents in wedding family photos?

Decide first whether all the parents are comfortable together. If they are, put every parent and step-parent in the parent photos. If not, take each group twice, once per household, and keep each new partner apart from the other household as well as the exes.

Should a step-parent be in the photo with the parents?

If they're part of the family, yes. They stand on the outside of the parent they came with. If the divorced parents are being kept apart, the step-parent is in their own partner's version of each photo, not the other parent's.

How many extra photos does a blended family need?

In our example of ten relatives, everyone together took 9 photos (about 18 minutes). Two separate households took 14 photos (about 28 minutes). An incomplete set of keep-apart rules produced 19.

Are half-siblings and step-siblings in the family photos?

Half-siblings are siblings and belong in every sibling photo. Step-siblings you grew up with too; step-siblings you barely know usually join only the big group with everyone on that side.
