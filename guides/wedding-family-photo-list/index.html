Going from 16 relatives to 28 added no photos: the extra aunts, uncles and cousins all go into the one "everyone on this side" group.

What adds photos is a new kind of group (grandparents, siblings who bring partners and kids) and anyone who shouldn't stand together. Each divorced pair kept apart turns several groups into two; see divorced parents in family photos. Big groups do take longer to pose, which is a question of minutes per photo, not of how many photos are on the list.

Photos worth adding only if they matter to you

The planner suggests these for most families but leaves them unticked, because plenty of couples skip them:

  • Couple with parents and grandparents. Three generations in one frame. Lovely if a grandparent is frail and this might be the last chance; otherwise it's close to the immediate-family photo.
  • Couple with siblings, and each partner with their own siblings. Popular with close siblings, and quick: no parents to gather.

And these you'd add as your own photos, because only you know they exist:

  • Couple with your own children, especially from an earlier relationship. Put it first and tick "needs to go first" for the kids.
  • A grandparent alone with the couple, if one side has two grandparents and one of them is the reason you're all here.
  • Godparents, a guardian, the aunt who raised you. If they're family to you, they belong on this list, not in the cocktail-hour candids.

What to leave off the formal list

  • Friends, roommates, the wedding party. Formals are for family because family is what you can't get later. Friends will be at the reception, and your photographer can grab them there.
  • "All the cousins" as its own photo, unless the cousins are close. They're already in the big group.
  • Every combination. At the planner's default pace each extra photo costs about two minutes for everyone still waiting. Five extra combinations add about ten minutes before anyone gets a drink.

Write names, not roles. "Aunts and uncles" means nothing to the person shouting across a lawn. "Aunt Sue, Uncle Bob" does. The planner works from names for this reason: every photo's call card lists exactly who steps in.

Questions

How many family photos should we plan at a wedding?

For most families, 7 to 19. The planner suggested 7 for a family with only parents and one sibling on each side, 13 for families with grandparents, aunts and cousins (whether 16 or 28 relatives), and 19 once a divorced pair had to be kept apart.

Who counts as immediate family in wedding photos?

Parents and step-parents, brothers and sisters, and their partners and children. Grandparents usually get their own photo, and aunts, uncles and cousins join the larger "everyone on this side" group.

Should cousins be in the family formals?

Yes, in the one big photo with everyone on that side. A separate cousins-only photo is optional; if you want one, take it right after the big group, while they're all still there.

Do we need a photo with both sets of parents?

Most couples take one. It's a good photo to take between the two sides, when one set of parents is finishing and the other is about to start. If either set of parents is divorced, it turns into two or four photos.
